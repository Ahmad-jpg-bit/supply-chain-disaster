How to Run a Supply Chain Disruption Simulation: Games, Tabletop Exercises and When to Use Each

March 9, 2026 · Ahmad Faruqi · 10 min read

When COVID-19 closed factories and ports in 2020, many organisations found that their plans for disruption had never been tested against a fast-moving, multi-tier crisis. Rehearsal is one way teams try to close that gap: working through a realistic scenario before a real one arrives. This article explains the main rehearsal formats, including tabletop exercises and game-based simulations, what each can and cannot teach, and how to run a simple disruption simulation for your own team.

We build a supply chain game (Supply Chain Disaster), so we say where it fits and where it does not. We do not have evidence that one format is better than another, and the choice depends on what you want people to learn.

What Is a Supply Chain Disruption Simulation?

A supply chain disruption simulation places participants inside a time-compressed crisis scenario, such as a port strike, a tier-1 supplier failure or a border closure, and asks them to make procurement, logistics and communication decisions. The core mechanism is a decision-consequence feedback loop: in a well-designed simulation, each action has a visible cost. Ordering aggressively during a shortfall can raise unit costs and create excess stock later, and waiting for certainty can mean waiting until stock has run out.

Simulations come in several formats:

  • Solo digital simulations: an individual works through a scenario alone and gets feedback after each decision. Good for self-paced practice at scale.
  • Team-based exercises and war games: two or more players take different roles (procurement, logistics, sales) and coordinate under shared constraints. Good for cross-functional alignment.
  • Facilitated scenario workshops: a moderator introduces events as teams adapt. Closer to a live drill without the operational risk.

Tabletop Exercise vs. Game Simulation: What's the Difference?

A tabletop exercise is a structured, facilitated discussion. A scenario is presented, for example "your primary semiconductor supplier has announced a six-week shutdown", and participants talk through how they would respond. Its strengths are surfacing communication gaps, process ambiguities and differing assumptions, and building shared understanding. Its limit is that the model of consequences lives in people's heads: if someone says "we would dual-source immediately", the group has to challenge that for itself.

A game-based simulation asks for specific decisions, such as quantities, timing and spend, and calculates the consequences. If you say you would dual-source, the model can charge a qualification cost, apply a lead time and use the supplier's price. Its limit is that the model only contains what its designers put in, and a solo game does not exercise team communication.

The two serve different learning objectives, and neither replaces the other:

Dimension Tabletop exercise Game simulation Live drill
Best for Coordination, communication, process gaps, decision rights Individual practice with quantities, timing and cost trade-offs Executing procedures with real systems and people
Consequences Discussed and judged by participants and facilitator Calculated by the model, within its assumptions Real
Feedback Facilitator and peers, often at the end Immediate, per decision Usually in a post-event review
Participants A group with a facilitator One player or many, depending on the product A cross-functional group
Effort and cost Facilitator and meeting time Software, plus time to play and debrief Highest: operational resources
Main limit No calculated consequences The model's assumptions; limited team dynamics if solo Cost, risk and scheduling

Facilitation and debriefing matter in every format. What people learn from an exercise depends heavily on how it is framed, how the discussion is run afterwards and whether findings turn into actions. A well-run tabletop can teach more than a poorly debriefed game, and the reverse is also true. We do not claim that games universally outperform tabletop exercises, and we are not aware of evidence for such a claim.

Operational Insight

In a tabletop discussion, a team may agree it would "increase safety stock immediately". In Supply Chain Disaster, a larger order has a visible cost in cash and holding cost, and the result shows how the quantity played out against demand. That makes the trade-off concrete for the player; it does not test whether your own organisation could actually execute the decision.

COVID-19 and Disruption Preparedness

The pandemic exposed several weaknesses that many organisations reported afterwards:

  1. Limited multi-tier visibility. Many teams knew their tier-1 suppliers but had not mapped tier-2 dependencies, so the first sign of a problem upstream could be a missed delivery.
  2. Distorted demand signals. When stores closed or customers changed behaviour overnight, point-of-sale data stopped reflecting underlying demand, and orders swung sharply.
  3. Reactive purchasing. Without a practised crisis procedure, some teams switched to expensive spot-market buying under time pressure.

Rehearsal is a plausible way to address these gaps, and many organisations now run scenario exercises. But we cannot show that organisations which recovered fastest did so because of earlier simulation training. Recovery depended on many things, including supplier relationships, inventory, product mix, finance and luck, and we are not aware of research that isolates training as the cause. Treat simulation as one tool among several, not as a proven predictor of resilience.

How to Design and Run a Disruption Simulation for Your Team

A useful simulation does not require enterprise software or a professional facilitation team. It needs a clear scope, a realistic scenario and a way to connect decisions to consequences. A practical framework:

Step 1: Define the Disruption Type and Scope

Choose a specific failure mode: port strike, supplier bankruptcy, demand spike, quality recall, border closure or logistics network disruption. Then decide whether you are simulating a single-tier impact (your direct suppliers only) or an end-to-end disruption (raw materials through customer delivery). Single-tier simulations are faster to design and easier to debrief. End-to-end simulations are more realistic and expose more interdependencies.

Step 2: Set Initial Conditions

Define the starting state: inventory levels, outstanding purchase orders, lead times, supplier capacity and cash reserves. Use real data from your organisation where possible, because a scenario built on your own operating parameters is more relevant than a generic one. If real data is unavailable, use clearly labelled assumptions.

Step 3: Run Several Decision Rounds

Structure the exercise around a few sequential decision rounds, each advancing the crisis by a week or a procurement cycle. In round one, the disruption is announced and participants make initial responses. In round two, the consequences of round one arrive and a secondary complication is introduced (a backup supplier that cannot scale, a logistics partner at capacity). In round three, participants work with the resources their earlier decisions left them and try to stabilise operations.

Step 4: Measure and Debrief with Data

Measure outcomes you defined in advance, for example service level (units delivered divided by units demanded), total cost (including expediting, spot purchases and holding), cash position and time to recover. Debrief by comparing decisions across participants and tracing how early choices and assumptions shaped later results. Then record actions: what you will change in your plans, contacts, contracts or data.

What a Simulation Can Let You Practise

We cannot show that a simulation improves real-world performance, so these are things it can let you practise and observe, not guaranteed results.

Demand Amplification

The bullwhip effect is the amplification of small demand fluctuations as orders move upstream. A simulation can let you generate it yourself: adding a buffer to an order because of uncertainty, repeated at each node, can produce order volumes that bear little relation to consumer demand.

Reactive vs. Proactive Procurement

Reactive buying can look efficient when suppliers are reliable and becomes expensive in a crisis. Premiums for expedited freight or spot purchases vary widely by event and market, so test them with your own cost data. A simulation with visible cost consequences lets you see how that trade-off plays out under time pressure.

Why Safety Stock Is Not Set-and-Forget

Standard safety stock formulas assume a reasonably stable lead time distribution. When lead times become volatile, the formula's inputs are out of date. A scenario that introduces lead time variability mid-way can show why coverage needs to be reviewed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a supply chain disruption simulation?

A supply chain disruption simulation is a time-compressed crisis scenario, such as a port strike or a supplier failure, in which participants make procurement and logistics decisions and see the consequences. It can be run solo, in teams or as a facilitated workshop.

How do companies prepare teams for supply chain crises?

Common formats are tabletop exercises, war games, live drills and game-based simulations. They serve different objectives: tabletop exercises are suited to coordination and decision rights, simulations to practising quantified trade-offs, and live drills to executing procedures. Facilitation and debriefing strongly affect what people take away from any of them.

Are games better than tabletop exercises for training?

Not universally. We are not aware of evidence that games outperform tabletop exercises across the board. Choose the format by the learning objective, and plan the debrief in either case.
