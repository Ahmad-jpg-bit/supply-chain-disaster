SCM Fundamentals: Navigating the 5 Pillars of Chaos

March 9, 2026 · Ahmad Faruqi · 10 min read

Supply chain fundamentals are usually taught through tidy frameworks, and the frameworks are useful as long as you remember what they are. This article walks through three common ways of organising the subject — five stages of a supply chain, a five-pillar grouping of SCM activities, and four supply chain strategy types — and then looks at SCOR, including how ASCM's current SCOR Digital Standard differs from the older Plan–Source–Make–Deliver–Return representation.

A note on status. The five stages and the five pillars below are explanatory groupings we use in this article. Textbooks and websites list different "stages" and "pillars", and no single list is a universal or mandated standard. SCOR, by contrast, is a published reference model maintained by ASCM, and the four strategy types come from published research (cited below).

What Are the 5 Stages of a Supply Chain?

One simple way to describe a supply chain is as five stages that follow the flow of goods, information and money from raw material to end customer. It is a simplification, but it helps show where disruptions can start and where interventions might help.

Stage 1: Sourcing and Procurement

This is where materials, components and services are acquired from suppliers. Procurement choices — single-source versus dual-source, spot market versus long-term contract — shape how exposed you are to a supplier problem before any crisis occurs. Sourcing concentrated in one supplier or region leaves less room to switch when something goes wrong.

Stage 2: Manufacturing and Production

Raw inputs are transformed into finished goods. Just-in-time scheduling is common here, and it depends heavily on the reliability of upstream procurement, so a stage 1 problem can reach stage 2 within days or weeks, depending on how much stock sits in between.

Stage 3: Inventory and Warehousing

Inventory is not only waste; it is also a buffer. Safety stock absorbs demand and supplier variability, and the right level depends on your service level target, the variability of demand and lead time, and the cost of holding stock. Lean inventories save money when conditions are stable and leave less cover when they are not.

Stage 4: Transportation and Logistics

Combining modes — for example ocean freight, rail and last-mile delivery — can lower cost in normal conditions and offer alternatives during a disruption. Reliance on a single route or mode is a concentration risk, as the 2021 Suez Canal blockage showed.

Stage 5: Customer Delivery and Returns

The final stage closes the loop: goods reach the customer, and reverse logistics handles returns, defective products and end-of-life processing. Sales and returns data feed back into forecasting, and distortion in that feedback is one of the causes of the bullwhip effect.

What Are the 5 Pillars of SCM? An Explanatory Grouping

The grouping below organises supply chain management into five areas of activity that need to work together. They are parallel disciplines, not sequential stages, and other authors draw the lines differently.

1. Information and Process Integration

A supply chain depends on the information that flows between its nodes. Integration means shared visibility: suppliers can see your demand plans, your logistics provider knows your schedule, and your planners know what is actually selling. Fragmented visibility is a common contributor to demand signal distortion.

2. Operations

The physical execution of supply chain activities — manufacturing, warehousing, picking, packing and shipping. Operational excellence is about removing costs that add no value while keeping the buffers that absorb variability.

3. Purchasing

Strategic purchasing goes beyond price negotiation. It includes supplier qualification, sourcing strategy, contract flexibility and total cost of ownership analysis. Buying on unit price alone can hide concentration risks that only become visible during a disruption.

4. Distribution

Getting the right product to the right customer at the right time through an economical route. Distribution strategy has to balance cost against service commitments, and that balance changes when a lane or port is disrupted.

5. Customer and Supplier Relationships

Supply chains are networks of organisations, not just flows of goods. Collaborative forecasting, information sharing and aligned incentives between buyers and suppliers can make a network more resilient.

What Are the 4 Types of Supply Chains?

The four types come from Hau Lee's 2002 article "Aligning Supply Chain Strategies with Product Uncertainties" (California Management Review, vol. 44, no. 3), which matches a supply chain strategy to two kinds of uncertainty: how uncertain demand is, and how uncertain supply is. The table is our summary of that framework, not a quotation, and the examples are illustrations rather than rules.

Type Demand uncertainty Supply uncertainty Main emphasis
EfficientLowLowLow cost: high utilisation, minimal inventory along the chain
Risk-hedgingLowHighProtecting against supply problems: pooled or shared safety stock, multiple suppliers
ResponsiveHighLowReacting quickly to changing demand: flexible capacity, short lead times
AgileHighHighCombining responsiveness with hedging against supply problems

Which type fits depends on the product and its supply base, not on the industry label. A company can run different types for different products, and a product can move between types as its demand or supply becomes more or less predictable. Matching the strategy to the uncertainty is the point; the more flexible types generally cost more to run.

SCOR: The Older Five-Process Model and the Current Digital Standard

SCOR (Supply Chain Operations Reference) is a process reference model, now maintained by ASCM, that gives supply chains a common vocabulary of processes, metrics and practices. Earlier versions described the chain with Plan, Source, Make, Deliver and Return (with Enable added in later versions), and the "five processes" description is still repeated on many websites.

ASCM's current framework is the SCOR Digital Standard (SCOR DS). ASCM describes it as organised around seven primary management processes: Orchestrate, Plan, Order, Source, Transform, Fulfill and Return. Orchestrate links strategy and enablement to the other six. Broadly, the older Make corresponds to Transform, and the older Deliver is split into Order and Fulfill. For the precise definitions, use ASCM's own SCOR DS documentation at scor.ascm.org; the five-process list above is the older representation, so check which version a course or an employer's reference materials use.

Reliability vs. Agility in a Port Blockage Scenario

A highly reliable supply chain runs consistent, predictable processes optimised for known conditions. An agile one gives up some consistency to respond to unknown conditions; earlier SCOR versions list both reliability and agility among their performance attributes. A blocked port is a useful test. A delay in inbound Source can carry through Transform and Fulfill, and the more your plan assumes things go as normal, the less room you have to adapt. Reliability without contingency planning leaves little room to absorb a shock.
